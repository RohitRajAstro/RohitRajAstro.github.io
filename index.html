Rohit Raj

Stellar astrophysics · SPARK Postbac Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian

I study how stars lose mass — and how well we can trust the measurements that tell us they do.

I am currently a SPARK Postbac Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics Harvard & Smithsonian, working with Dr. Andrea Dupree and Dr. Paul Cristofari. My main project models the surface mass ejection behind Betelgeuse’s 2019–2020 “Great Dimming”: using non-LTE radiative transfer, I simulate how a dense outflow moving through the chromosphere reshapes the Mg II line profiles seen in HST/STIS spectra, constraining the ejection’s velocity and mass. In parallel, I am building a neural spectral emulator to forward-model how magnetic spot distributions imprint themselves on cool-star spectra — groundwork for the next generation of high-resolution spectrographs like G-CLEF on the GMT.

Before Cambridge, I spent two summers at UC Santa Cruz with Prof. Raja GuhaThakurta studying resolved stellar populations in M31 and M33. That work grew into my first first-author paper — a study of Wolf-Rayet stars in M33, now under review at ApJ — and an awarded Keck/OSIRIS program to follow it up. It also taught me the question that quietly runs through everything I do: how much of what we measure is the star, and how much is the blend, the fiber, or the model? I built an HST-based crowding pipeline after discovering that neighboring stars were contaminating our spectra, and earlier showed that DESI’s fibers systematically bias stellar velocities in crowded fields. Understanding — and correcting — what our instruments actually see is, to me, as fundamental as the astrophysics itself.

I came to physics the long way. I grew up in Hanspur, a village in Bihar, India, where my school had no lab and I had no internet until grade 10. I taught myself English and then physics from online courses, and eventually earned a full-tuition scholarship to study physics at Juniata College (with an exchange year at the University of Leeds). That road left me with two convictions: that curiosity is universal but opportunity is not, and that I owe a debt forward. I still run a free learning center for ~50 students in my hometown, mentor young researchers wherever I can, and volunteer with astronomy outreach around Cambridge.

Away from the terminal, I read widely, bike and hike whenever New England weather permits, and think (and occasionally write) about geopolitics, scientific ingenuity, and how ideas originate and travel — the history of how we came to know fascinates me as much as what we know.

I will be applying to astrophysics PhD programs for Fall 2027. If our interests overlap — stellar atmospheres, spectroscopy, time-domain astronomy, or the machine-learning tools that connect models to data — I would love to hear from you at rohit.raj@cfa.harvard.edu.
