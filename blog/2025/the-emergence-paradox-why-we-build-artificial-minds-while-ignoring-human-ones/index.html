Intelligence is precious. It is rare in big chunks, but small amounts are scattered across many species. It reminds me of the stellar initial mass function in astronomy: massive stars are incredibly rare compared to the abundance of less massive, Sun-like stars.

Defining “intelligence” is difficult, but we all have an intuitive grasp of the phenomenon. It’s the ability to think, reason logically, create, and critique. But deeper than the definition is the mechanism. Is intelligence an emergent phenomenon?

In physics, emergence is a beautiful concept. If you take a large number of simple things — let’s say pixels — where each unit only varies in color and intensity, you get an emergent image. Vary them over time, and you get a video. The “video” doesn’t exist in the single pixel; it exists in the collective behavior. Similarly, in physical systems, a bunch of particles interacting in a simple pattern can lead to complex macroscopic behaviors that seem to have no relation to the properties of the individual particles.

We are currently building neural networks that do repetitive tasks and look for patterns. From this, we hope the phenomenon of intelligence will “emerge,” even if we don’t fully understand why. If that turns out to be the case, welcome to the Emergence Club. We see the steps, but the result feels like magic. (If you want a bigger challenge and AI is boring now, try understanding Dark Matter or Dark Energy — that field is still wide open).

A similarly fascinating phenomenon is the emergence of order out of chaos. In physics, we often encounter systems where we cannot predict future behavior with certainty. These systems have a sensitive dependence on initial conditions — popularly known as the Butterfly Effect.

The patterns traced by these systems look random, chaotic, and like a “no-brainer,” until suddenly, an interesting outcome in the form of order emerges. Specifically, what we humans perceive as order (since we are biased toward repetitive patterns). We can see the mathematical core steps, but how exactly that order crystallizes from the chaos is often more complicated than simple probability or chance.

The Irony of the AI Mirror

Here is my wonder: Will AI, which gains its super-intelligence from this chaotic, emergent process, be able to help us understand emergence?

It would be the perfect irony. A machine explaining the concept of emergence, while being a result of it.

I hope it explains its own emergence, too. Self-reflection is a skill most humans have developed, yet we still don’t understand our own “source code.” We have subconscious processes driving us; we don’t always know why we took a specific step. The origins of instinct and creativity are still largely black boxes.

Essentially, we are trying to recreate something we don’t totally understand (intelligence), through a process we don’t fully master (deep learning emergence), to develop a Super Intelligence.

This sounds like a recipe for a blunder. But this is also how science progresses — we stumble forward in the dark. However, when it directly impacts humanity, we usually take great caution. I hope the people developing AI are holding onto that caution.

The Missing Billions

Looking at the billions of dollars pouring into the AI industry, it pleases my heart that we care about intelligence. I am optimistic about an era of abundance.

But I have to ask: Why has nobody globally thought about investing this heavily in human intelligence?

There are so many young kids and students — in remote villages, in underfunded schools — waiting for an opportunity to learn. We could accelerate the development of AI by getting smart, hardworking kids from across the globe to bring fresh perspectives to the field.

It is a well-known fact in science that diversity of perspective is key to solving hard problems. Who is putting billions into infrastructure to create the next generation of scientists, engineers, social entrepreneurs, and philosophers for the Age of AI?

We don’t just need coders. We need people who are well-versed in AI but deeply aware of its downsides. We need people to cross-check the biases. We need high critical thinking, strong empathy, and emotional intelligence.

The upcoming era will bring challenges we haven’t seen yet. The crisis of cheap distractions and the erosion of critical thinking is already severe; AI could explode this problem into something much bigger. Between the isolation of the pandemic and the wave of algorithmic content, we are already fragile.

We need to prepare a generation of thinkers who can handle this.
