Symmetry; What picture comes to your mind after reading the word? For me, it’s the rhythmic arrangement of leaves along a stem, the mesmerizing allure of fractals, and the intricate balance embedded in the equations of physics.

Defined by Cambridge dictionary as “The quality of having parts that match each other, especially in a way that is attractive, or similarity of shape or contents”, symmetry is a captivating phenomenon. Simple yet enigmatic, it extends its influence across disciplines, invoking notions of beauty, order, and balance in different fields.

In the vast canvas of knowledge, symmetry finds its place in chemistry, unraveling the secrets of molecular structures and reactions. It guides the understanding of living organisms’ intricate forms in biology and shapes the visual compositions in the world of art. Architects draw upon symmetry to craft structures that blend strength, stability, and beauty, while physicists turn to it to decode the laws governing our universe.

Venturing into the depths of physics, the significance of symmetry is as deep as the gravitational well of a black hole. Attempting to encapsulate its entirety, even within the confines of a book, is a daunting task, yet we strive for a mere glimpse in this article.

Symmetry, a transformation of a system/equation that leaves it in the same state. In a way, a symmetry is a way of changing a system without actually changing it — a perplexing beauty. For example, consider repeating an experiment after time transformation/translation, or say after a few days, with the same conditions,the result will remain unaltered. And it can be verified by anyone, anywhere in this world. (Such repeatability is a hallmark of scientific identity unlike pseudoscience !). Similarly, the spatial translation is the invariance of a system under a shift in space. I.e. If you move a ball from one point to another in space, the laws of physics governing its motion will be the same.(of course unless you move it to a singularity where the physical laws itself breaks, forget the ball). There are other symmetries like, Rotational symmetry, Gauge symmetry(more abstract type), but the idea behind all of them is very similar. While you might be thinking, this idea is very easy and obvious, this is one of the most profound concepts in almost all of physics. There are not many theories that have applications in physics across its fields. It’s a profound idea, woven into the fabric of physics theories spanning classical mechanics to quantum mechanics and beyond, including the intriguing world of supersymmetry.

Someone who first truly grasped the profound implications of symmetry in the realm of physics was Emmy Noether. Born in 1882 in Erlangen, Germany, Noether was a mathematician whose groundbreaking contributions laid the foundation for a fundamental connection between symmetries and physical laws. In the early 20th century, physics was undergoing a revolution, with classical mechanics making way for the mysterious and counterintuitive world of quantum mechanics. Amidst this intellectual upheaval, Emmy Noether’s theorem emerged as a beacon of clarity.

Noether’s theorem, published in 1918, established a profound link between symmetries and conservation laws. The theorem tells that for every continuous symmetry in a physical system, there exists a corresponding conservation law. In simpler terms, the symmetries we observe in the physical laws of nature are intrinsically connected to the preservation of certain quantities. For example, the conservation of energy. If the laws of physics governing a system remain unchanged over time (time translation symmetry,I showed earlier), Noether’s theorem tells us that energy is conserved. This insight resonates across the entire spectrum of physics, from the predictable orbits of planets to the dynamic dance of subatomic particles. (Energy is always conserved, locally, not discussing the cosmic scale here).

We all come across these conserved quantities like energy, which nobody actually knows what it actually is but a number that always comes out to be conserved in closed local systems. It is very interesting to see these quantities emerging from mathematical formalism, Noether’s theorem. Similarly there are other familiar conserved quantities, like momentum, angular momentum and charge, which originates similarly.

Now, why all the forces in nature are conserved (non conservative forces are just results of our ignorance), nobody knows but this generality of symmetry and conservation laws across the topics definitely has something deeper and might be hinting at a deeper unity, beckoning towards the ongoing quest for a unified theory in physics. In past, symmetry has been one of the important factor for scientists in pointing them towards unification, like the beautiful symmetry electromagnetism equations beholds after the unification, and maybe we will complete its last bit by discovering the monopole, which seems like more obvious to me that many things we have already discovered.

All of them are very elegant and interesting to people in their respective field and even beyond.
