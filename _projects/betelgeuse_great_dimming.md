In late 2019, Betelgeuse — one of the brightest stars in the sky — faded so dramatically that people wondered aloud whether it was about to explode. It wasn't. The leading explanation is a **surface mass ejection**: a huge parcel of the star's atmosphere was launched outward, cooled into dust, and temporarily blocked our view.

At the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, working with Dr. Andrea Dupree and Dr. Paul Cristofari, I model what that ejection did to the star's *chromosphere*. HST/STIS ultraviolet spectra taken through the dimming show strikingly asymmetric **Mg II line profiles** — a fingerprint of gas in motion. Using the Rybicki–Hummer non-LTE radiative transfer code, I simulate the emergent spectrum as a dense outflow travels through the atmosphere, and I have reproduced the observed line asymmetries while constraining the ejection's velocity and the mass it carried away.

Why it matters: red supergiants like Betelgeuse seed galaxies with heavy elements through exactly this kind of episodic mass loss, yet the mechanism is one of the least understood pieces of massive-star evolution. Catching one star in the act — with time-resolved UV spectra and a physical model to interpret them — is a rare chance to measure it directly.

I presented this work at the 247th AAS meeting (January 2026), where it received the **Chambliss Astronomy Achievement Student Award**; the first-author paper is in preparation for *ApJ*.
